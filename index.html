NATIONAL CIVIC INFRASTRUCTURE AUDIT

Locked Out of Play?

Investigating socioeconomic disparities, loose-fill ground barriers, national sanitary catchments, and capital grant targeting across 2,239 Australian public playgrounds and 25,624 public toilets.

ACT I — MACRO & MUNICIPAL PROVISION

The Geography of Play: Capital Clusters & Per-Capita Contradictions

Across Australia's 2,239 documented OpenStreetMap playgrounds, raw infrastructure is heavily concentrated along coastal metropolitan belts. Victoria (729 sites), New South Wales (578), and Queensland (486) account for 80.1% of national play spaces, while 64.4% sit within 55 km of state capital CBDs.

The Per-Child Normalization Paradox

Normalizing playground counts by ABS child population (playgrounds per 10,000 children aged 0–14) reveals a striking geographic bifurcation across Australia's 547 Local Government Areas (LGAs).

First, 253 of 547 LGAs (46.3%) record zero playgrounds (shaded grey), forming a vast inland documentation and infrastructure void across pastoral WA, NT, and outback QLD.

Second, among active councils, high-volume outer-metro growth belts (Casey, Brisbane, Wyndham) dilute to 3–6 parks per 10k children due to booming youth populations, whereas small-population rural shires in Victoria's Wimmera and South Australia exceed 20+ parks per 10k children.

ACT II — SOCIOECONOMIC EQUITY

The Socioeconomic Divide: ABS SEIFA Deciles & The Regional Gap

Joining LGA playground counts with the ABS Index of Relative Socio-economic Advantage and Disadvantage (SEIFA) exposes a steep socioeconomic gradient between Australia's poorest and wealthiest municipalities.

The 4.7x SEIFA Gradient: Disadvantaged councils (Deciles 1–3) average just 1.68 playgrounds per LGA, compared to 7.94 playgrounds per LGA across advantaged municipalities (Deciles 8–10).

State Volume vs. Sanitary Co-Location: Comparing mean playgrounds per active LGA (left) against suburb accessible toilet co-location % (right) shows that even high-volume states leave 35–60% of play spaces without nearby accessible toilets.

ACT III — MICRO-LEVEL ASSET QUALITY

Anatomy of Play: Loose-Fill Surfaces & State Auditing Proportions

For children using wheelchairs or walkers, ground surfacing determines whether a park is inclusive or off-limits. Examining the 423 surface-audited sites in our dataset shows an overwhelming reliance on loose-fill materials across every state.

The Loose-Fill Mobility Barrier

Among playgrounds with recorded ground materials, loose-fill Woodchips & Bark (44.9%) and Sand (43.5%) account for 88.4% of audited sites, whereas unitary Impact Rubber represents just 4.3% (18 sites).

Cross-tabulating surface material against verified wheelchair ratings confirms the physical bottleneck: 60% of woodchip sites and 54% of sand sites are rated completely inaccessible (no), while unitary surfaces achieve significantly higher compliance.

ACT IV — NATIONAL ACCESSIBILITY & SANITARY CATCHMENTS

The Dwell-Time Barrier: Wheelchair Audits & National Toilet Co-Location

Inclusive play requires both physical playground mobility and nearby accessible toilets so families can stay for more than 30 minutes. By linking all 2,239 playgrounds with the 25,624 facilities in the National Public Toilet Map, we evaluate national family dwell-time infrastructure.

Nearly Two-Thirds Restrict Wheelchair Users

Among Australian playgrounds with a verified wheelchair audit outcome, only 36% (21 sites) are fully accessible (yes).

Another 22% (13 sites) offer only partial access (limited)—typically step-free perimeter paths paired with loose-fill equipment zones—while 42% (24 sites) completely lock out wheelchair users (no).

The Compounding Deficit: When a playground lacks both unitary surfacing and a nearby accessible toilet, families of children with mobility needs are effectively excluded from public recreation.
ACT V — EMPIRICAL POLICY CASE STUDY

The Capital Response: NSW 'Everyone Can Play' Grants (140 Projects, $19.08M)

Did state capital grants fix these socioeconomic and regional deficits? Linking all 140 funded projects ($19.08M) from the NSW Everyone Can Play register (2018/19–2021/22) to ABS SEIFA council deciles reveals that competitive grant allocation favored affluent metropolitan councils.

Regressive Capture Across SEIFA Deciles

Aggregating grant funding across ABS SEIFA Deciles and funding rounds exposes a stark policy blind spot: Decile 1 councils (the most disadvantaged 10%) received $0 and 0 grants across all four financial years.

Conversely, Decile 10 (the wealthiest 10% of councils) captured $3.44M across 25 projects—more than Deciles 1 through 4 combined—because competitive co-funding rules favor high-revenue metropolitan councils with dedicated grant-writing teams.

The Round 4 Divergence: Capital Scale vs. Regional Stagnation

Tracking mean project funding across all four rounds reveals how early regional balance gave way to metropolitan scale. In 2018/19 and 2019/20, Regional & Rural councils secured larger mean grants ($123.6k–$123.8k) than Metropolitan councils ($100.7k–$106.0k) to offset regional freight and construction costs.

By Round 4 (2021/22), that pattern reversed sharply: Metropolitan mean grants surged +42.6% to $151,173 while Regional & Rural grants dropped to $108,207, opening a $42,966 per-project metro lead.

The Co-Funding Bottleneck: Mandatory dollar-for-dollar council matching rules allow high-revenue metropolitan councils to pitch large destination parks while capping what smaller rural ratebases can request.

Repeat Winners: Institutional Capture at the Top

Ranking individual municipalities by cumulative funding confirms that capital grants concentrated repeatedly within the same well-resourced councils: the top 12 councils alone captured $7.33M (38.4% of state funding) across 54 separate grants.

Affluent Ku-ring-gai Council (SEIFA Decile 10) secured a state-high $837,166 across 6 grants, followed by Bayside ($693k) and Penrith ($689k). Nine of the top 12 recipients are metropolitan councils, whereas inland and Decile 1 shires remained completely unrepresented.

Policy Takeaway: Replacing competitive co-funding with needs-indexed baseline allocations is essential so disadvantaged and regional councils are not outcompeted by repeat metropolitan winners.

Conclusion: Breaking Australia's Triple Lock of Play Inequity

Across all five acts, our audit reveals a compounding triple lock of spatial and mobility exclusion: disadvantaged councils average 4.7× fewer playgrounds than affluent municipalities, while 88.4% loose-fill surfacing and a 43.8% accessible toilet catchment void physically bar children with mobility needs from existing parks. Rather than correcting these structural deficits, competitive co-funded grants reinforced them—channeling $3.44M into the wealthiest Decile 10 councils while leaving the most disadvantaged Decile 1 communities with $0.
